IBM has built a prototype chip with roughly **100 billion transistors** on an area the size of a fingernail — twice the density of its previous state-of-the-art from 2021. The breakthrough uses a new architecture called a **nanostack**, which vertically stacks transistors in two layers on a silicon chip rather than shrinking them further, as traditional approaches to Moore's Law have hit fundamental quantum mechanical limits.

Compared to IBM's prior architecture, chips built with the nanostack can do up to 50% more work in the same time and are up to 70% more energy efficient. IBM Research director Jay Gambetta called it "a meaningful leap forward," and expects nanostacked chips to be widely deployed in data centers within a decade, where their efficiency could transform energy management. Dan Hutcheson of TechInsights said the development "puts another 10, 15 years on the roadmap."

IBM's approach, known as a complementary field-effect transistor (CFET), builds transistors layer by layer rather than bonding prefabricated layers together — allowing more precise alignment and better performance. The company achieved this using processes kept below 400°C to avoid melting lower-layer connections. IBM will partner with semiconductor manufacturers to bring the design to production, and anticipates deployment across GPUs and CPUs.

Why it matters: As AI compute demand explodes, IBM's nanostack offers a path to keep transistor density growing for another decade — ensuring the hardware foundation for future AI systems continues to improve when traditional shrinking has reached its limit.
