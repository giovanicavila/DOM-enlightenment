The European Parliament voted on June 16, 2026 to formally amend the EU AI Act for the first time since it entered into force in August 2024, passing the Digital Omnibus package with 423 votes in favour, 57 against, and 174 abstentions. The amendments represent the most significant recalibration of Europe's AI regulatory framework to date, addressing industry concerns about compliance timelines while introducing new prohibitions on the most controversial AI misuse cases.

The centerpiece of the reform is a推迟 of high-risk AI system obligations: standalone high-risk systems (used in hiring, education, law enforcement, and similar settings) will now face compliance requirements from **December 2, 2027** rather than August 2026. AI systems embedded as safety components in regulated products (medical devices, machinery) get until **August 2, 2028**. The watermarking obligation for AI-generated content is also delayed to December 2, 2026 for systems already on the market. The extension gives companies breathing room while standards and support measures are finalized, but rights organizations including EDRi and Access Now warned the delays weaken safeguards and create regulatory loopholes.

In a significant new prohibition, the amended Act bans "nudifier" AI systems that generate non-consensual intimate imagery or child sexual abuse material, effective December 2, 2026. The ban covers both providers and deployers of such systems. The amendments also resolve a long-standing regulatory overlap by clarifying that AI-enabled machinery products need only comply with sectoral safety legislation rather than duplicating AI Act requirements, and provide clearer definitions of "safety components" so that assistive AI functions are not automatically classified as high-risk. The legislation requires formal approval by the Council of the European Union before entering into force, which is expected before August 2, 2026.

**Why it matters:** The EU AI Act amendments strike a pragmatic balance — giving industry more time to comply with high-risk obligations (the provisions most costly to implement) while sending an unequivocal signal that certain AI harms are unacceptable, establishing a regulatory template that other jurisdictions are closely watching.
